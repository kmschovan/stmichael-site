About Us

Frequently Asked Questions

The questions we hear most often — about who we are, our liturgy, and how to join us — answered plainly.

Q. What is Saint Michael & All Angels Catholic Church?

A. Saint Michael & All Angels is a Roman Catholic community-in-formation in Denison, Texas, serving North Texas and southern Oklahoma under the jurisdiction of the Personal Ordinariate of the Chair of Saint Peter. Unlike most other Catholic churches in the area, we are not under the Diocese of Dallas; however, we are in full communion with the diocese, and we work in close cooperation with local parishes, serving the Catholic faithful together in a complementary way. The Personal Ordinariate of the Chair of Saint Peter is a canonical jurisdiction within the Catholic Church that covers the United States and Canada. It is one of three similar ordinariates in the English-speaking world, and operates directly under the Pope and the Vatican Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith. Each ordinariate has many local churches within its canonical jurisdiction — Saint Michael & All Angels is one of those churches, not yet large enough to be canonically established as a parish.

Q. Is Saint Michael & All Angels a Catholic Church?

A. YES! Saint Michael & All Angels is a Roman Catholic church, which is part of the Personal Ordinariate of the Chair of Saint Peter. This Ordinariate operates directly under the Pope of Rome and the Vatican Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith (DDF). All members of Saint Michael & All Angels are Roman Catholics. We are in full communion with the Diocese of Dallas and with the United States Conference of Catholic Bishops (USCCB), and our community is listed with the Ordinariate parish finder. If you have any questions or concerns about this, feel free to contact the Diocese or the USCCB.

Q. What is the Ordinariate?

A. The Ordinariate is a special canonical jurisdiction (like a super-diocese) within the Roman Catholic Church. The Personal Ordinariate of the Chair of Saint Peter spans the entire United States and Canada, overlapping regular territorial dioceses but applying only to certain persons and parishes, and is governed by our own bishop in Houston, Texas, who is familiar with our Medieval English Patrimony. This is somewhat like a personal prelature or religious order. Saint Michael & All Angels is established and formed as part of this jurisdiction. If we likened the Ordinariate to a religious order, then think of Saint Michael & All Angels as one community within that order.

Q. How large is the Ordinariate?

A. There are three ordinariates in total. The first is called the Personal Ordinariate of Our Lady of Walsingham, founded in 2011, with jurisdiction in the United Kingdom. The second is the Personal Ordinariate of the Chair of Saint Peter, founded in 2012, with jurisdiction in the United States and Canada — Saint Michael & All Angels is under this ordinariate, which is growing rapidly. The third is the Personal Ordinariate of Our Lady of the Southern Cross, founded in 2012, with jurisdiction in Australia and Oceania. To see all the communities in our ordinariate, visit the Ordinariate parish finder.

Q. Who may attend Holy Mass at Saint Michael & All Angels?

A. Anyone may attend Holy Mass at Saint Michael & All Angels, and all are welcome. One does not need to be a member to attend Mass here. All of our sacraments are valid and legal Roman Catholic sacraments, and our priest is a validly and legally ordained Roman Catholic priest. Any Catholic may receive the sacraments offered by our clergy. Non-Catholics are welcome to attend and pray with us as well, but should not receive communion.

Q. What form of liturgy does Saint Michael & All Angels use?

A. There is only one Holy Mass in the Catholic Church, but that same Holy Mass can take on different liturgical rites and forms. We use Divine Worship, a third form of the Roman Rite, sometimes called the Medieval "English Form" or "English Use" of the Roman Rite. The Roman Rite has three main forms. The first is the Ordinary Form, also known as the 1970 Roman Missal — the usual Mass celebrated in vernacular languages, typically seen in most Catholic churches around the world, including here in North Texas. The second is the Extraordinary Form, also known as the 1962 Roman Missal — the all-Latin form that was the norm until 1970, still celebrated in some churches. The third is Divine Worship: a unique liturgy based on sacral English and traditions brought back into the Catholic Church by Anglican converts. It has many similarities to how Catholics worshipped in England during the Middle Ages, before the Protestant Reformation, and is designed to appeal to English-speaking people. You may have noticed that the Our Father is exactly the same at Divine Worship Mass as in the Ordinary Form — that heritage runs deep.

Q. Are the liturgies at Saint Michael & All Angels a separate rite?

A. No. This is a very common question. Many people are under the impression that we have our own rite apart from the Roman Rite. This is not the case. Saint Michael & All Angels uses Divine Worship, which is part of the Roman Rite — a special third form brought back into the Catholic Church by Anglican converts. Its roots reach back to the Medieval English liturgy preserved by Anglicans for centuries; it has now returned to the Catholic Church and is 100% fully Roman Catholic. We believe all the same doctrines that Roman Catholics have always believed, and we keep the same traditions Roman Catholics have always kept. Because we are part of the Roman Rite, we fall under the same Code of Canon Law. Our bishop is a member of the U.S. Conference of Catholic Bishops, his cathedral is in Houston, Texas, and he answers directly to the Pope and the Vatican Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith (DDF).

Q. Who may receive communion at Saint Michael & All Angels?

A. Any Catholic who is properly disposed and in a state of grace may receive the sacrament of Holy Eucharist at our Divine Worship Mass. We encourage reception of the sacrament of reconciliation (confession) before Mass if there is any doubt or question about a state of grace. Non-Catholics should not receive communion. If you believe there is a special circumstance that would make an exception to this, please discuss it with our priest privately before receiving communion. Those who will not be receiving communion may still approach the altar rail for a blessing. This is done by placing one's hands on opposite shoulders, arms crossed, to signal to the priest that one will be receiving a blessing in place of the Holy Eucharist. The priest will always understand what this means.

Q. How is communion received at Saint Michael & All Angels?

A. For those Catholics who are properly disposed and in a state of grace, communion is received on the tongue while kneeling at the altar rail. This will always be the case, unless one has a physical disability that makes this impossible, in which case another accommodation will be made. We use the method of intinction for communion, wherein the Eucharistic host is dipped into the precious blood by the priest immediately before it is placed on the communicant's tongue. We find that this method of receiving communion puts emphasis on the sacred act itself, drawing attention to what is happening: God Almighty, hidden under the mere appearance of bread and wine, is coming into physical communion with human beings. Many Catholics who have received communion this way often report a heightened sense of reverence and solemnity. It should also be noted that according to Rome, reception of communion on the tongue, while kneeling, is the default way (the normal way) of receiving communion throughout the Catholic Church worldwide. Communion given in the hand, while standing, is a practice that is allowed in the United States only by special permission from Rome, on the condition that communicants may still receive on the tongue while kneeling if they so choose. While many Catholic churches in the United States distribute communion in the hand, we at Saint Michael & All Angels do not; bodily position is accommodated according to medical needs.

Q. Why do some women at Saint Michael & All Angels wear hats or veils?

A. The tradition is Biblical in origin. It comes from St. Paul's instruction in 1 Corinthians 11:1-16, and it has been the tradition of Catholic churches in antiquity — both East and West. Wearing a veil or hat is not mandatory, but many women do it as a sign of Mary-like humility, especially when in prayer. Some women prefer lace veils or regular shawls; others prefer hats, as is consistent with English custom. Christian head-covering differs from head-covering in other religions: in Christianity, women cover their heads primarily in the presence of God during prayer and worship, usually for no other reason. While head-coverings are not required, we fully support women who choose to keep this Biblical custom. We are a "head-covering friendly" community. We ask only that all head-coverings be modest and not distracting, and that all women be respected in their personal choice on this matter. Some cover, some do not — both choices are to be respected here. If you have concerns, please bring it up with our priest privately.

Q. How should I dress at Saint Michael & All Angels?

A. We do not have a strict dress code. Some enjoy putting on their "Sunday best," while others (for various reasons) do not. Your attire does not need to be formal, but overly casual attire is not truly respectful toward the Lord when we are at Mass. It is important to maintain a non-judgmental atmosphere when it comes to clothing. We ask only that, in all things, personal modesty is kept a top priority — this applies to both men and women. We ask that worshipers cover their full torso, including shoulders, and avoid deep necklines; that legs be covered at least to the knee; and that regular shoes or proper sandals be worn. In all things, come clean, modest, and suitable for worship of Almighty God.

Q. Why does the priest turn his back to the people during Mass?

A. During the Holy Mass, the priest never "turns his back to the people," but rather he faces the Lord together with the people, leading them into worship. This position is called ad orientem, which in Latin means "facing toward the east." The "east" is not always literal, and the priest does not always face literal east; it comes from Scripture: "For as lightning cometh out of the east, and appeareth even into the west: so shall the coming of the Son of man be" (Matthew 24:27). The Latin term versus Dominum — "facing the Lord" — is also used. The idea is that the priest always leads the people into the worship of God. This position is the usual one for Eastern Catholics and had been the normal position for Western Catholics up until the 1970s, when the position of versus populum ("facing the people") was introduced. Many High Church Anglicans preserved the ad orientem tradition, and in keeping with that Medieval English Patrimony, we do the same at Saint Michael & All Angels.

Q. Why are some Ordinariate priests married?

A. The Catholic Church has always allowed married men to become priests (though not bishops) — this is especially noticeable in the Eastern Catholic churches. In the West, celibacy became the norm for candidates to the priesthood nearly 900 years ago, which is why most Western Catholic priests are celibate. That said, Rome still allows married men to become Catholic priests in the Western Church in special circumstances, with special permission from the Pope. Some Ordinariate priests are celibate and some are married; the married priests within the Ordinariate were once married Catholic laymen — usually converts from Anglican ministry — who were given special permission by the Pope to be ordained as Catholic priests. Ordinariate bishops, however, are always celibate, as is the norm for all bishops throughout the Catholic Church, East and West.

Q. Do Catholics in the Ordinariate honor Mary?

A. Yes, Catholics in the Ordinariate are regular Catholics. Honor of Mary is part of both our Catholic Faith and our Medieval English Patrimony. To be clear, no Catholic worships Mary — the worship of Mary is forbidden in the Catholic Church. Rather, Catholics honor Mary and show her great love, even asking for her prayers and intercessions, much like we would ask our friends and family for theirs. Catholics in the Ordinariate are no different. For nearly a thousand years, Mary has been honored under the title of Our Lady of Walsingham, recognized by both Catholics and Anglicans alike. In AD 1061, Our Lady appeared to Richeldis de Faverches, a pious English noblewoman in the village of Walsingham in Norfolk, England; Lady Richeldis built a small structure named "The Holy House," which later became a shrine and place of pilgrimage. In 2015, Pope Francis raised her sanctuary to the status of a minor basilica. Catholics in the Ordinariate are very fond of Marian devotion, and many frequently pray the rosary, wear the brown scapular, and visit approved Marian shrines.

Q. Does one need to be a member of the Ordinariate to be part of Saint Michael & All Angels?

A. No. Church membership is entirely different from Ordinariate membership. Many Ordinariate communities have "mixed membership," meaning some members are part of the local Diocese while others are part of the Ordinariate — Saint Michael & All Angels is like this too. Becoming a member of the Ordinariate is not required to be part of our community, though many of our members are also Ordinariate members. Ordinariate membership fully unites Catholics to the Medieval English Patrimony we celebrate here and places one under the canonical jurisdiction of the bishop of the Ordinariate in Houston, Texas. This has some advantages for those who wish to receive other sacraments using our liturgy — confirmation and matrimony, for example. If you think you might be eligible for Ordinariate membership, or are unsure, simply talk with Father Randall about it privately.

Q. How does one become a member of Saint Michael & All Angels?

A. If you would like to become a member of our community, speak with Father Randall after Mass or call the parish office at 903-821-7511. If you are not yet Catholic and would like to join the Catholic Church through our community, you will want our OCIA program (the Order of Christian Initiation of Adults, sometimes called RCIA) — a journey of formation and rites that culminates in receiving the sacraments at the Easter Vigil. You can learn all about it on our OCIA page, and inquiries are welcome at any time of year.
